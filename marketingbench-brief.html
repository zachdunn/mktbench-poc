Why publish a benchmark

The strategic logic, in one paragraph each.

1Whoever defines the eval defines the standard. Harvey converted its proprietary asset — access to real legal workflows and expert reviewers — into public infrastructure. Frontier labs now optimize against Harvey's definition of good legal work, and its benchmark became the neutral yardstick the industry reports against. The equivalent move in marketing is open.

2The white space is real. The vertical-benchmark map (Vals AI's lineup is the de facto index) covers legal, finance, healthcare, coding, and web search. There is no marketing or lifecycle-marketing benchmark from anyone with domain authority — only generic academic efforts with no practitioner anchoring.

3Klaviyo is uniquely positioned to build it. The workflow surface (what marketing work actually looks like), the aggregate performance data (to make synthetic environments distributionally honest, and — later — to validate rubrics against real outcomes), the agency ecosystem (the credibility role law firms play for Harvey), and a product reason: the benchmark is the measurement layer for exactly the work Composer does.

4It answers the trust question, not just the capability question. The #1 buyer objection to autonomous marketing agents is "will it burn my list, my deliverability, and my brand while chasing its metric." Two of this benchmark's layers — escalation judgment and the do-no-harm layer — measure precisely that, and no published benchmark in any vertical measures either.

Sierra: the publisher to watch

Why one row of that table gets its own section.

Market overlap is direct, and their benchmark motion is fast

Sierra (Bret Taylor's AI agent company) sells enterprise customer-experience agents — the same category as Klaviyo's Customer Agent, and τ-bench's original domains are retail and airline customer service. Their benchmark cadence: τ-bench (2024, tool-agent-user interaction under written policy), τ²-bench (2025, dual-control — agent and simulated user both act), τ-knowledge (March 2026, agents navigating a 698-document knowledge base; frontier models still under 40% Pass^1). Three benchmarks in roughly twenty months, each one pulling the industry's definition of "reliable agent" further onto Sierra's turf.

Three implications for this project:

  • They proved the exact playbook. A vendor-published benchmark, openly released with code and leaderboard, became the reliability metric frontier labs cite in model launches. Sierra is the existence proof that a product company — not a lab, not academia — can own the yardstick for its own category.
  • Their methodology is worth borrowing, openly. Two pieces in particular: policy-compliance grading (an agent that takes an against-policy action fails regardless of how good the interaction looked — the ancestor of our authority envelope and escalation slice) and pass^k (success on k consecutive independent runs — a reliability metric, not a capability metric). Enterprise buyers deploy agents that succeed every time, not agents that succeed once; we adopt pass^k for shippable rate, reported as pass^1 and pass^4 alongside single-run scores.
  • The white space will not stay open. Sierra's domains have marched retail → airline → telecom → banking knowledge work. A τ-marketing domain — or a Sierra-style benchmark from any adjacent CX/CRM vendor — is a plausible next move. The first credible, practitioner-anchored marketing benchmark published sets the standard everyone else must position against; second place argues with the rubric.

The central design problem — and its resolution

Why marketing can't just copy the legal benchmark, and the architecture that follows.

In law, expert consensus is ground truth. In marketing, it isn't — the market is. Two excellent marketers given the same brief produce different campaigns, and both can be right; the true score (incremental revenue) is observable only in production, confounded, and noisy. A benchmark that pretends expert rubrics fully capture quality gets dismissed by practitioners; one that only scores fuzzy "quality" gets dismissed by researchers.

The resolution: marketing work decomposes into layers with very different verifiability — and each layer is graded the way it can actually be graded. The key insight: most "marketing judgment" becomes objectively gradable when criteria are written as known failure modes ("did the winback exclude recent purchasers?") rather than ideal answers.

Two-tier scoring follows directly. A campaign with a broken discount code fails no matter how good the copy — but among shippable campaigns quality gradations are real. So the headline metric is shippable rate (all-pass on L1–L3 gates plus hard L4 failure modes, reported as pass^1 / pass^4), with a quality score on L4–L5 reported conditional on shippable. All-pass realism without flattening the quality signal.

Anatomy: tasks, universes, planted truth

The Harvey task structure, rebuilt for marketing — with a generation trick law can't use.

Four-part tasks

Instructions (~50 words, realistically underspecified, "ecomm director to lifecycle manager") · Environment (closed universe of brand files) · Output (reviewable work product, structured where possible) · Verification (30–60 atomic binary criteria, each linked to specific files).

Closed-universe environments

Product catalog with margins & inventory, 24 months of profiles/events/performance, existing flows & segments as structured JSON, brand guidelines, deliverability reports, stakeholder communications carrying buried constraints, rendered email templates and site captures.

Generator-emitted ground truth

Universes are synthetic, calibrated to Klaviyo's aggregate vertical data. Faults are injected programmatically, so every quantitative criterion has an exact generator-computed answer — and a new seed regenerates the universe, making the private held-out set and contamination refresh nearly free.

The two-signature rule

Every planted issue has a definition-level cause and a performance-level symptom that reconcile quantitatively — so grading distinguishes agents that found the symptom from agents that diagnosed the mechanism, and the universe survives experts pulling threads.

Two universes built (88 files, deterministic)

Alma Botánica — mid-size DTC skincare: 342k profiles, AOV $62, 74-day repurchase cycle, subscriptions, a no-discounts-on-the-hero-line rule. Carries 8 data-level planted issues (flow collision, winback targeting happy subscribers, expired welcome code, quiet-hours timezone bug, VIP segment rot…), 4 render-level issues visible only in captured light/dark/mobile screenshots (an invisible dark-mode CTA; a discount code trapped in a hero image), and an escalation brief pair.

Meridian Travel Goods — premium DTC luggage (Away-like category dynamics, fully fictional): 910k profiles, AOV $285, a 3.8-year repurchase cycle, gift-heavy Q4, a strict zero-discount policy. It proves the framework's core claim: the same fault classes produce different correct answers in a different category. A 90-day winback is sensible for serums and absurd for suitcases; cart-recovery discounting is tactics for most brands and brand damage here; "purchaser = owner" fails for gifted luggage. Generic best practices score poorly by design — rubrics are authored per-universe.

What Klaviyo's own data can feed into it

The benchmark's environments are synthetic — but nearly every part of it gets sharper when grounded in datasets only Klaviyo holds. All uses are aggregate and anonymized; data-use terms review is a gating open question.

Support & CX tickets — the registry of what actually goes wrong

Tickets where customers are trying to fix a marketing problem — or are worried an agent did something out of band — are an empirical catalog of real failure modes, written in the customer's own words. They feed four layers at once: planted issues (the universes' faults should mirror what really breaks, at the frequency it really breaks — no more inventing failure modes), nuisance severity weights (harms that generate tickets are empirically worse than harms that don't), escalation triggers (the situations where customers wished a human had been asked), and duty-to-notice scenarios (problems that sat visible-but-unflagged before someone complained). "My agent sent a campaign I never approved" tickets are, quite literally, the ground truth for the benchmark's trust layers.

The novel pieces

What this benchmark measures that no published benchmark does — each labeled by provenance.

Coding transplant

Account invariants — pass-to-pass for marketing

SWE-bench's quiet masterstroke: everything that worked before the patch must still work after. Here, whole-account invariants (no sends to suppressed/non-consented profiles, no frequency-cap breaches, no overlapping enrollments, no OOS promotion) are checked after every task's changes, whatever the task asked. Invariant violations are gate failures — a task cannot be "shippable with some collateral damage."

Coding transplant

The simulated-send nuisance ledger

Execution-based grading, transplanted: the harness executes the agent's proposed end-state against the fully-known synthetic population over a 60-day horizon and emits a deterministic ledger of who receives what, when, under what consent status. Severity-weighted harm events aggregate to a collateral damage score — exposure to harm is computed, never judged, and recipient response simulation is deliberately excluded from graded metrics.

Novel

Duty-to-notice

No coding benchmark penalizes an agent for scrolling past an unrelated bug. Marketing practice has the norm (you read the send log, you saw the 6am SMS, you say something) — and the harness makes it gradeable: because file access is logged, omission demerits fire only when the agent demonstrably encountered the evidence and stayed silent. To our knowledge, unmeasured by any published benchmark in any domain.

Novel

Escalation precision and recall

Tasks where the correct deliverable is to stop and seek a human — conflicting stakeholder instructions, data anomalies, consent gray zones — under a written authority envelope mirroring Composer's approval gate, with "use your judgment" pressure tests. The keystone: unlabeled clean controls, lookalike tasks where acting is correct, so hedging fails as surely as barreling ahead. Reported as a precision/recall pair, never singly.

Klaviyo-only moat

Outcome-grounded rubric validation

Harvey can never know whether the memo worked. Klaviyo observes realized outcomes at scale — so L4 judgment criteria can be empirically tested against the historical record (do campaigns satisfying criterion X outperform matched campaigns that don't?), and non-predictive criteria get cut. It converts the benchmark's weakest layer, expert opinion, into its strongest claim. No legal or finance benchmark can say that sentence.

Klaviyo-only moat

Backtest task families

Held-out tasks where the agent chooses among decisions whose outcomes are already known — real, anonymized A/B tests and campaign variants. Noisy per-item, informative in aggregate, and impossible to contaminate: the answers live in private data. Phase 3, with survivorship and significance caveats handled in the methodology paper.

Phases

Harvey's sequencing, adapted: dataset and methodology before any leaderboard. This is an order of operations, not a schedule — timing is a separate conversation.

Phase 0

Design partners & groundwork framework, spec & 2 sample universes built

Recruit 2–3 agencies and 2–3 in-house lifecycle leaders as task decomposers and rubric validators — the credibility role law firms play for Harvey, and a distribution channel. Open the neutral-grading conversation early (Vals or academic): their submission-normalization standards shape the harness. Legal review of publishing compliance criteria; naming review (the existing Klaviyo performance benchmarks are a collision to manage and an asset to calibrate against).

Phase 1

Pilot — prove the grading stack

~46 tasks across the two gate-heavy categories (account audit, flow design) plus the visual-input and escalation slices, run on both universes. Frontier-model baselines and a human-expert baseline (time + shippable rate) on a subsample. The pilot's real deliverable is judge-vs-human agreement numbers per criterion type — the evidence that decides how much weight phases 2–3 can carry. Universe stress-test: two senior marketers paid to break the data's internal consistency before any task is authored against it.

Phase 2

v1 public launch

4–6 universes spanning vertical × size × maturity, 8 task categories, 300–500 tasks. Open dev set + private held-out set (regenerated by seed). Methodology paper with inter-rater agreement, judge calibration, and the corollary map. Named research partners. Deliberately no leaderboard yet — invite audit, contribution, and lab engagement first.

Phase 3

Leaderboard + the layers nobody can copy

Third-party-run leaderboard with normalized submissions (pass^1/pass^4, quality-conditional, collateral damage, escalation P/R, harm-adjusted value). Outcome-validated rubrics and backtest task families as the differentiating announcement. Visual-output grading via the ArtifactsBench-style pipeline, gated on the phase-1 vision-judge agreement numbers. Longitudinal (multi-week) task families begin design.

North star

A published paper

The longer-term goal: Klaviyo publishes research the way Sierra does — τ-bench and its successors are papers with open code and leaderboards, and that's what made them citable infrastructure rather than vendor collateral. This project generates several genuinely publishable contributions: the judge-vs-human calibration evidence from the pilot, the duty-to-notice mechanism (unmeasured by any benchmark in any domain), the simulated-send nuisance ledger, the revenue-weighted capability index, and the outcome-validated-rubric methodology. A methodology paper (arXiv, then a venue) plus the open harness is the credibility ceiling for the whole effort — it's the difference between "Klaviyo made a marketing quiz" and "Klaviyo defined how this work gets measured."

Open questions that shape everything

  • Composer and the grade-your-own-homework problem. Does Klaviyo's own agent appear at launch, and what happens if it doesn't win? Options: dataset-first (Harvey's move), a public commitment to publish Composer's scores whatever they are, or v1 scoped to foundation models. Third-party grading is table stakes for whichever path.
  • Data-use terms. Can aggregate Klaviyo data calibrate synthetic universes — and later validate rubrics — under current customer agreements? This gates both the distributional-honesty claim and the phase-3 moat.
  • Environment access model. File-system-only (Harvey's model, phase 1) vs. a mock-API environment (phase 2) — determines how much of L2 grading is machine-checkable, and how neutral the harness looks.
  • Naming. "MarketingBench" is a placeholder; the collision with Klaviyo's existing performance-benchmark reports needs resolving (capability benchmark ≠ performance norms), ideally in a way that makes the existing reports a calibration asset rather than a confusion.
  • Governance. Advisory board composition (agencies + brands + a lab + academic) — cheap insurance against the benchmark reading as a press release.

The posture on low scores

Every serious vertical benchmark launched with headline numbers that looked bad — the best finance agent scored 46.8%; frontier models are under 40% on Sierra's newest domain. Low scores are the finding: "the work is harder than the hype, and here is the measuring stick." That framing must be decided before the first baseline run, not after.
